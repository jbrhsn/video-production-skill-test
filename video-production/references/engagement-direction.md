# Engagement direction

Read before writing a new transcript or substantially revising a storyboard. These editorial principles draw on the workspace essay “Why Infographic Reels Are So Hard to Ignore”; they are design hypotheses, not universal psychological laws or guarantees of reach.

Define the audience's concrete situation, one memorable takeaway, the opening promise, and how the ending fulfills it. Give the first image meaning before narration finishes. A hook can be a visible comparison, a question, a recognizable problem, or a surprising observation supported by the source. Avoid vague suspense and unsupported superlatives.

Build a sequence of discoveries. Each beat should change what the viewer understands: introduce a question, show evidence, explain a mechanism, apply it, or resolve a tension. Deliver useful answers throughout. Keep enough context visible to follow the reasoning; an unlabeled chart that conceals essential meaning is not a good curiosity gap. Let a completed relationship register before replacing it.

Use one primary relationship per beat. Stage complex explanations and qualifications instead of removing nuance. Make motion express the relationship: growth for quantity, alignment for comparison, a connected path for sequence, or a transformation for cause and effect. Keep colors and object identities consistent. Vary staging and shot scale where it helps; do not impose a change every fixed number of seconds.

Optional storyboard direction fields:

- `viewer_question`: what the viewer wants to understand here.
- `new_understanding`: what this beat adds.
- `payoff`: how it resolves an earlier promise or supplies a useful insight.
- `continuity_to_next`: which object, action, question, or spatial relationship connects the next beat.

These are useful prompts, not required fields or a scene template. A video need not use a hook/body/CTA formula, and every scene need not contain a new hook. A short video can form a single argument; a longer horizontal explainer may use chapter-level questions and payoffs.

Have visuals demonstrate relationships, narration interpret them, and captions preserve access to the spoken explanation. Avoid competing headline/caption/diagram paragraphs. Retain accurate captions rather than substituting a few keywords for accessibility. Review muted for the basic visual argument, audio-only for explanatory structure, then together for attention conflicts.

Use everyday situations that fit the audience. End with a precise takeaway worth remembering or sharing; add a next action only if it naturally follows. Verify hook claims, chart scales, and evidence qualifications. Earn attention through clarity and useful discoveries, not fear, fake certainty, or delayed answers.
